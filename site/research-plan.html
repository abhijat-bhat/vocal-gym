Verdict at a glance

The practice-routine shell is familiar. The parts that make Vocal Gym publishable and defensible are the generators that cannot break raga or tala rules, the conformance coach and the personal voice model.

Novelty

High in four modules

Guaranteed-valid exercise, jathi and kalpana swara generation with a creativity dial; a kalpana swara tutor; phone-only personal strain baselines tied to warm-up choice. Raga conformance is medium-high: recognition is crowded, coaching-grade conformance is not.

Startup potential

The shared core: a raga–tala constraint engine

Every Carnatic exercise is a sequence that must satisfy two grammars at once. The tala fixes how long it is and where its boundaries fall. The raga fixes which swaras may appear and in which direction. Vocal Gym treats both as finite-state constraints and asks every generator to sample only inside their intersection. Creativity is a separate dial.

Tala
A sequence of angas: laghu |L with L ∈ {3, 4, 5, 7, 9} by jati, dhrutam O = 2, anudhrutam U = 1. Seven families × five jatis give 35 talas, from 3 aksharas (Tisra Eka) to 29 (Sankeerna Dhruva) [4]. For dance the engine adds Tisra (1 + 2), Khanda (2 + 3) and Misra (3 + 2 + 2) Chapu. Nadai g sets matras per akshara; speed (kalam) sets swaras per akshara.
Raga
An arohana list and an avarohana list. A step up must land on an arohana swara and a step down on an avarohana swara. Vakra ragas and anya swaras need explicit allow and deny lists (planned for the full engine).
Exercise
Notes or syllables x1…n with durations, subject to a constraint set C: exact length, karvais that never cross an anga line, fixed start, fixed landing (eduppu or sam).
p_T(x) ∝ exp( score(x) / τ ) · 1[ x ∈ C ] score(x) = (1 − c) · F(x) + c · N(x) c = T / 2, τ = 0.3 + 0.45 T, T ∈ [0, 2] reach(t, s) = OR over moves m from state s at time t of reach(t + dur(m), s') (backward pass, exact) sample: at (t, s) choose among moves with reach(t + dur, s') = true, weights exp(score / τ)

Because every partial sequence the sampler builds can still be completed, no output ever violates C, at any temperature, with no rejection and no backtracking. The alankara generator goes one step further and replaces OR with log-sum-exp in the same table, which gives exact samples of whole patterns from pT (forward filtering, backward sampling). This is the same family of ideas as Markov constraints (Pachet and Roy, 2011) and grammar-constrained decoding for language models (Geng et al., 2023) [61][62], specialised to raga and tala.

Temperature is the performer's creativity. Each generator scores a choice twice. F measures familiarity, how much it resembles what a teacher or listener expects: for alankaras, frequencies learned from the 35 lines of the tala alankara sheet [71]; for kalpana swaras and jathis, named conventional devices such as runs, janta, beat-aligned groups and the mora. N measures novelty: named unconventional devices that are still legal, such as leaps, zig-zags, syncopation, off-beat groups and shaped yatis. At T = 0, c = 0 and only familiarity counts; at T = 2, c = 1 and only novelty counts. T also widens sampling through τ, which never reaches zero, so asking for something new always gives something new. The rules are not part of the score, so no temperature can buy a violation.

        Module A · Alankaras across the 35 talas and every raga class

        The source. The first pattern for every tala comes from the 35 Tāla Alankāra-s sheet [71]. It writes the first ascending and first descending line of each alankara in Mayamalavagowla, for seven tala families × five jatis, and gives four instructions: fill the following lines in sequence up to tara Sa at least, then reverse and descend to madhya Sa; the notation is first speed, one swara per unit of the tala; second speed is two swaras per beat and third speed four, up to the sixth; and the alankaras can be sung in any melakarta. The seven alankaras found in most books are its default-jati members [1][2].

        Algorithm

        1. Variation 0 is the sheet. The sheet's line is read as scale degrees with durations and anga lines. Every melakarta is sampurna in both directions, so the same degrees give the sheet's pattern in all 72. The tests reproduce all 35 first lines, ascending and descending, note for note in Mayamalavagowla, and all ten lines of 1.1 Chaturashra Dhruva, which the sheet writes out in full.
        2. Shift through the raga. Ascending lines start on successive arohana swaras until a line reaches tara Sa; descending lines mirror them from tara Sa down to Sa [3]. A pattern spanning s swaras gives 9 − s lines each way in a seven-note raga. That matches the row count the sheet prints for every one of the 35 talas, from 2 rows (span 7, as in Sankeerna Dhruva) to 6 rows (span 3, as in Tishra Matya).
        3. Janya ragas get the sheet line, adapted. Degrees map to arohana swaras on the way up and avarohana swaras on the way down. When a step would break a rule (an upward turn landing on Kambhoji's N₂, which appears only in descent), a dynamic programme over the raga's swaras finds the closest legal line: it minimises distance from the direct mapping plus a penalty for changing the contour, and keeps the line inside its window. Changed swaras are underlined in teal and the page labels the line an adaptation, not the sheet.
        4. New pattern means a pattern not yet shown. For the same raga and tala, each request samples a fresh motif of the same length (by default with the sheet's span), compares it with the sheet line and with every pattern already shown for that raga and tala, and accepts it only if it differs in at least a quarter of its slots (one slot in talas of five aksharas or fewer). The history is kept per raga and tala, so Previous and Next step back through it. If a small tala runs out of distinct patterns, the demo says so instead of repeating one: Tisra Eka with a span of three has only four.
        5. Creativity. F is the smoothed log-frequency, over the 35 sheet lines, of three features of each move: the step (by size and direction, or a restart to Sa at an anga line), where a karvai falls (inside an anga or at its end) and whether the contour turns. N rewards leaps of a third or fourth, turns, crossing an anga line without restarting on Sa, and karvais that syncopate inside an anga. Exact sampling draws the whole pattern at once, so the familiar choice early on cannot force an awkward leap at the end. Measured over 1,248 new patterns, a pattern made at T = 0 is on average more conventional than 38% of the sheet's own lines, at T = 1 more conventional than 2%, and at T = 2 less conventional than all of them.

        Module B · Jathi creator, guide and judge

        A jathi is arithmetic that has to dance. This module teaches the rules, lets a student write a jathi in a precise text format, checks the arithmetic exactly, scores the craft and says what to change. It can also generate examples at any level of creativity.

        B1 · Terms

        Adavu
        The basic unit of Bharatanatyam movement; adavus are combined to form dance sequences [77].
        Sollukattu
        A phrase of rhythmic syllables linked to a unit of movement and recited by the nattuvanar, for example tai ya tai ya, tai hat tai hi, tat tai tam [77]. Konnakol names standard groups by length: tha ka (2), tha ki ta (3), tha ka dhi mi (4), tha dhi gi na thom (5), tha ka dhi mi tha ka (6), tha ka dhi mi tha ki ta (7), tha ka dhi mi tha ka jho nu (8), tha ka dhi mi tha dhi gi na thom (9) [73].
        Jathi
        A passage of sollukattu set to the tala for pure dance, recited by the nattuvanar and closed by a teermanam.
        Korvai
        A string of adavus set to sollukattu that ends with a teermanam [19][77].
        Teermanam
        "The final knot" [19]: a closing phrase performed three times [77]. Here the three statements must be identical and separated by equal karvais.
        Karvai
        A silent or held matra, written ",". It counts exactly like a syllable.
        Yati
        An arrangement of phrases whose lengths draw a shape [74]. Six types: sama, vishama, mridanga, damaru, gopuccha and srotovaha [72].
        Eduppu
        Where the passage starts relative to sam [75][76]: sama eduppu on sam, or a few counts (usually 2, 4 or 6) before sam (atita) or after it (anagata) [75].

        B2 · The tala grid and the one equation

        An avartanam has A aksharas grouped into angas; nadai g is the number of matras in an akshara (tisra 3, chatusra 4, khanda 5, misra 7, sankeerna 9); kalai k counts each akshara k times. Bharatanatyam draws mostly on Adi (8), Rupaka (6), Misra Chapu (7 = 3 + 2 + 2) and Tisra Triputa (7) [18]; Adi and Rupaka are the usual talas for adavus [77]. Everything a jathi must satisfy reduces to one equation.

        matras per avartanam C = A · k · g span from eduppu to sam M = N · C − e e > 0: starts e matras after sam (anagata); e < 0: starts |e| before (atita) slots to fill S = M + 1 the last syllable sounds on sam (S = M if the jathi ends exactly at sam) teermanam 3u + 2q phrase of u matras, two gaps of q the equation body + yati + 3u + 2q = S

        Example: Adi, chatusra nadai, two avartanams from sam: C = 8 × 1 × 4 = 32, M = 64, S = 65. A teermanam of tha dhi gi na thom (u = 5) with gaps of 3 takes 21, which leaves 44 for body and yati.

        B3 · Rules and constraints

        Rules 1 to 5 are hard: the judge will not call a jathi correct until they hold. Rules 6 to 9 are craft, and the judge scores them.

        1. Exact span. Every syllable and karvai between the eduppu and the landing is counted. The total must equal S; one matra too many or too few is an error.
        2. Landing. By default the final syllable sounds on sam. A jathi may instead end exactly at sam, or land n matras after sam, for example on the eduppu of the song that follows; this is declared, not guessed.
        3. Eduppu. The first syllable sounds at the eduppu. An atita eduppu begins in the previous cycle, so the span grows by |e|; an anagata eduppu shortens it by e.
        4. Teermanam. Three identical statements with two equal gaps, ending on the landing. The gaps may be zero.
        5. Bars tell the truth. Phrases may cross anga lines, which is where cross-rhythm comes from, but every bar a student writes must sit on an anga line (|) or the end of an avartanam (||).
        6. Beat and speed. Cross-rhythm should resolve within three or four aksharas; a change of speed (for example from 4 to 8 syllables per akshara) should start on a beat and stay within double the nadai.
        7. Idiom. Use standard sollu groups and their suffixes, and put strong syllables (tha, dhi, tat, tai, thom) or phrase starts on the beats. End the teermanam on a closing syllable such as thom, dhit, tam, nam or jham.
        8. Yati. Grow or cut each phrase from its neighbour so the shape is audible: tha ka dhi mi tha ki ta / dhi mi tha ki ta / tha ki ta.
        9. Danceability. Leave karvais where the dancer can settle (60–95% of matras sounding), no unbroken run longer than two avartanams, phrases of at most nine syllables so each maps to an adavu.

        B5 · Guide: write your own jathi in eight steps

        1. Frame it. Choose tala, nadai, avartanams, eduppu and landing, then compute C, M and S. Adi, chatusra, 2 avartanams, sama eduppu, landing on sam: S = 65.
        2. Choose the teermanam. Pick a phrase u and a gap q. With tha dhi gi na thom and q = 3, each statement plus its gap is 8 matras, exactly two aksharas, so all three statements start on beats: 3 × 5 + 2 × 3 = 21.
        3. Subtract. 65 − 21 = 44 matras for the body and yati.
        4. Lead into it with a yati. A gopuccha cut from tha ka tha ki ta: tha ka tha ki ta / ka tha ki ta / tha ki ta = 5 + 4 + 3 = 12. The body is then 32 matras, one whole avartanam.
        5. Fill the body anga by anga. Laghu (16): tha ka dhi mi · tha ka jho nu · tha ka dhi mi · tha ka jho nu. Dhrutam (8): tha , dhi , tha ka dhi mi. Dhrutam (8): tha ka tha ki ta · tha ki ta.
        6. Write it in JathiScript with bars where you expect anga lines, so the judge can check your counting.
        7. Judge it. Fix the gate first: the judge names the line and the number of matras. Then raise the craft scores.
        8. Recite, then dance. Play it with the tala clicks, recite it with the tala in your hands, then dance it slowly before bringing it up to speed.

        B8 · The generator
        
  
        The generator enumerates every combination of teermanam phrase, gap (0 to 4) and yati block that fits the equation exactly, chooses one by temperature, and fills the body with sollu groups whose lengths sum to what is left. Familiarity rewards teermanam statements that start on beats, beat-length groups, repetition, no yati or a sama yati, and a teermanam of about a third of the span. Novelty rewards shaped yatis (gopuccha, srotovaha, mridanga, damaru), gapped teermanams, odd-length groups and phrases that cross the beat. Every generated jathi is exported to JathiScript ("Open in editor") and judged by the same rules a student faces.
        
  
        Verified. 12,417 generated jathis (35 talas and 3 Chapu talas × nadai 3–5 × 1–2 avartanams × eduppu −2, 0 and +2 × seven yati settings × three temperatures) summed exactly, had the requested yati shape and a threefold teermanam; 1,947 spans that were too short were refused with a message. 2,452 of them were exported to JathiScript and judged: all passed the gate, scoring 71 to 100 (median 88).

        Module C · Kalpana swara tutor
        
  
        
  
        What the tradition asks. Kalpana swaras improvise in swara syllables within the raga and tala and must return to the eduppu, the point in the cycle where the composition line resumes [9]. Rounds grow from simple to complex; strong performers vary their ending notes and finish with a korvai [10]. A common drill, kuraippu, repeats rounds that halve in length around a focal note: one avartanam, half, a quarter [12]. Korvais that return to an offset eduppu depend on simple divisibility arithmetic [11].
        
  
        Temperature is the singer's creativity
        
  
        A round is built from named devices. At low temperature the generator sings like a careful student: runs, janta, dattu and sequences that start on beats, held notes on Sa and Pa, and a mora at the end, patterns listeners recognise and can follow. At high temperature it sings like an adventurous performer: leaps, arpeggios, zig-zags, syncopated holds, groups that start off the beat and excursions into the upper and lower octaves. At every temperature each step obeys the raga's direction rules, the round has exactly the requested length, and it lands on the chosen swara just before the eduppu.

        Context adds to both scores. A device that starts on a beat or fills whole beats adds familiarity; one that starts off the beat or has an odd length adds novelty.         How a round is built
        
  
          
    
        1. Reachability. From the opening swara, mark every (slot, swara) state that some sequence of legal devices can reach.
          2. 
    
        2. Ending. Either free, or a mora: a phrase of two to four swaras ending on the landing swara, sung three times with equal karvais. A mora is offered only if the body before it can still step legally into its first swara. Low temperature prefers a stepwise mora whose statements start on beats; high temperature allows skips, off-beat statements or a free ending. The demo can force either.
          3. 
    
        3. Backward feasibility. For the body, mark every (slot, swara) from which the ending can still be reached exactly.
          4. 
    
        4. Sampling. Choose devices left to right with weights exp(score / τ), score = (1 − c) F + c N, among devices that keep the round finishable. There is no backtracking and no rejection.
          5. 
  
        
  
        Measured over 6,165 rounds across all 86 ragas: in rounds of eight slots or more, unconventional devices supply 1.7% of the swaras at T = 0, 24% at T = 1 and 41% at T = 2. Every round has the exact length, ends on its landing swara and obeys the direction rules; 1,282 rounds ended in a mora with three identical statements and equal gaps.
        
  
        Curriculum
        
  
          
    
        1. Echo. The tutor sings a half-avartanam phrase that lands on the eduppu; the learner repeats it. Scored on swara accuracy and timing.
          2. 
    
        2. Fill the slot. The learner improvises exactly k aksharas and must land. Feedback gives the landing error in matras and any out-of-raga swaras.
          3. 
    
        3. Focal-note rounds. Kuraippu on a nyasa swara: one avartanam, then half, then a quarter.
          4. 
    
        4. Devices. The learner is asked for a specific device (janta, dattu, a sequence, a mora) and the tutor checks that it appears and stays in the raga.
          5. 
    
        5. Creativity ladder. The same slot sung at rising temperatures: first with conventional devices only, then with one unconventional device per round, then freely. The tutor reports the share of unconventional devices, so creativity becomes something a student can practise and measure.
          6. 
    
        6. Korvai. The jathi solver proposes korvai lengths that reach the eduppu; the learner sings one.
          7. 
    
        7. Free rounds with critique. Raga adherence, devices used, variety and landing, summarised in plain language.
          8. 
  
        
  
        Listening pipeline
        
  
        On-device pitch estimation (PESTO or a small CREPE, fine-tuned on Carnatic data) [63][64][46]; the tonic is known because the app plays the drone; the contour is converted to cents and segmented into constant-pitch notes and stationary points [49][50]; swara tokens are aligned to the app's own tala grid; the scorers then report adherence, landing error, rhythmic evenness, devices used, repetition and korvai validity.
        
  
        A small new idea: learners sing swara names, so a seven-word keyword spotter can hear "ga" while the pitch tracker hears R₂. Comparing the two catches a classic beginner error. No prior system in this review checks name against pitch.

        What the literature says, taken together
        
  
          
    
        • Fatigue shows up differently in different people [29], and models trained on one person lose accuracy on another [20]. A personal baseline is required, not optional.
          • 
    
        • Neural embeddings carry fatigue information once they are normalised per recording and smoothed over time [20].
          • 
    
        • No study found uses a standardised daily singing probe recorded on a phone in an ordinary room.
          • 
    
        • The validated label (VFI) is too long for daily use; a short form needs its own validation.
          • 
    
        • No system closes the loop from a measured condition to a chosen warm-up and then measures whether the choice helped.

        Proposed method: Voice Check
        
  
        
    
        Probe (90 s, sung to the app drone)
        Sustained "aa" on Sa twice for 4 s; three soft "oo" onsets on upper Pa; an "ng" siren from lowest to highest comfortable note; a messa di voce on Sa; Sarali Varisai 1 in akaram at a fixed tempo.
        
    
        Features
        Smoothed cepstral peak prominence, harmonics-to-noise ratio, spectral tilt, pitch stability in cents on sustained notes, time to stable pitch at onset, highest note reached softly (a proxy for rising phonation threshold), range; early-layer WavLM or wav2vec 2.0 embeddings normalised per recording [65][66]; context: hours awake, vocal load and vocal dose since waking [30], sleep, illness flag, room noise, microphone.
        
    
        Model
        Hierarchical Bayesian: a population prior with person-level effects, giving personal z-scores against rested days; a small head mapping embeddings to self-reported strain with person-specific calibration; conformal prediction intervals so the app says when it is unsure [67]; inference on the device; federated averaging for the population prior [68].
        
    
        Labels
        A three-item daily short form derived from VFI items (to be validated), a 0–10 effort rating, and monthly ratings by a teacher or speech-language pathologist.
        
    
        Warm-up prescriber
        A contextual bandit (Thompson sampling) over warm-up protocols with a rule layer that cannot be overridden: high strain allows only SOVT work and a shorter session. Reward is the change in the post-warm-up probe plus self-rated readiness.
        
    
        Evaluation
        Within-person rank correlation with self-report, leave-days-out validation, calibration of intervals, cross-device robustness, and randomised n-of-1 crossover trials of bandit against fixed warm-ups.
        
    
        Safety
        Wellness feedback, never a diagnosis. Hoarseness that lasts beyond about four weeks, pain or loss of voice prompts a referral to an ENT or speech-language pathologist, in line with the AAO-HNS hoarseness guideline (Stachler et al., 2018) [69].

        Proposed method: raga conformance coach
        
  
          
    
        1. Clean input. The app synthesises the tanpura, so it knows the drone signal exactly and can subtract it from the microphone with an adaptive echo canceller before pitch tracking.
          2. 
    
        2. Pitch and tokens. PESTO or CREPE fine-tuned on Saraga-Carnatic-Melody-Synth [46]; cents relative to the known Sa; CPN/STA segmentation into swara tokens with gamaka descriptors [49].
          3. 
    
        3. Three scorers. Note level: per-raga, per-swara intonation distributions flag a swara sung where the raga does not place it. Transition level: the raga automaton plus a small language model over swara tokens flags varjya swaras and vakra violations by surprisal. Phrase level: a likelihood ratio of the chosen raga against its allied cohort (pairs taught together, such as Kalyani and Yamuna Kalyani, Kambhoji and Yadukulakambhoji, Darbar and Nayaki, Arabhi and Devagandhari) reports drift [37][38].
          4. 
    
        4. Training data at scale. Counterfactual violations: take valid phrases and inject controlled errors (a varjya swara, an allied-raga phrase, a flattened gamaka, a swarasthana off by a semitone), then resynthesise with gamaka-aware synthesis [17] or pitch-shift real audio. This yields labelled negatives without thousands of hours of annotated learner mistakes.
          5. 
    
        5. Teacher-calibrated tolerance. Because teachers set different thresholds [51], each student inherits their teacher's tolerance profile, adjustable by bani.
          6. 
    
        6. Evaluation. AUROC and F1 per error type on synthetic and real learner audio; event F1 with a collar for localisation [51]; agreement with teachers; under 150 ms latency on mid-range phones.
